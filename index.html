What a Qubit Is

This video explains what a qubit is: the two basis states, superposition, the complex amplitudes that describe it, and what happens when you measure one.

What happens in it

A qubit is a system with two states, and its state is two complex numbers — one weight for each possible outcome. That looks like four real numbers, but two of them turn out not to matter, and the two that remain are exactly the two angles needed to place a point on a sphere. That sphere is the Bloch sphere, and it is how the rest of this page draws a qubit. The video closes on what a measurement does: it returns one of the two values, with probability set by the weights, and leaves the qubit in whatever it returned.

02

Single-Qubit Gates

A single-qubit gate is a rotation of the Bloch sphere. Shine resonant light on the atom and the arrow turns; how far it turns is set by how hard and how long you drive it, and the axis it turns about is set by the phase of the light and by how far off resonance you are. An X gate is half a turn about the x axis; a Hadamard is a quarter turn that takes a pole to the equator. Everything else is the same machinery with different numbers.

Throughout this page the qubit is a &sup8;⁷Rb atom held in an optical tweezer, and the two levels are its hyperfine clock states. Every number in tab 03 — the linewidth, the saturation intensity, the detuning — is a rubidium number.

What happens in it

The video starts from a two-level atom and asks what laser light does to it. The light is an oscillating electric field; it couples the two levels at a rate called the Rabi frequency Ω. Combine that with the detuning δ — how far the laser sits from resonance — and you get a single axis in space. The state then simply precesses about that axis, at a rate set by its length. Everything else follows: on resonance the axis lies on the equator and the state sweeps pole to pole, so a pulse of the right length flips the atom. That pulse is the π pulse, and it is the X gate.

The CZ Gate on Two Atoms

To entangle two atoms they must interact with each other. To do that we take them to a very excited Rydberg state, where two atoms a micron apart shift each other's energy so strongly that they cannot both be excited at once. That is the blockade, and it is what makes the gate. Both atoms are flooded by the same beams to achieve this state.

05

Movement Extraction

We have simulated videos of a neutral-atom quantum computer shuttling atoms around a grid — animations of a syndrome-extraction cycle, not footage of hardware. This tool turns one of those videos back into the instruction list that produced it: a spreadsheet saying which atoms were picked up, when, and where they were put down. Then it runs the process backwards, rendering that spreadsheet into a new video and putting the two side by side, so you can see whether the answer is right rather than take anyone's word for it.

Where the traps come from

The tweezers are made by acousto-optic deflectors. An AOD is a crystal with a sound wave running through it; the sound acts as a moving diffraction grating, so a laser passing through comes out deflected by an angle set by the radio-frequency tone driving the crystal. Drive it with several tones at once and you get several beams at once — one trap per tone.

The machine crosses two of them. One AOD fans the light out into columns, a second, rotated by 90°, fans it into rows, and the light passes through both — so a trap appears at every intersection of a driven column with a driven row. Ten x tones and six y tones do not give you sixteen traps; they give you sixty, in a 10 × 6 block. That is exactly the X0 block in the example below, and it is why the workbook records a move as a list of x_tones and a list of y_tones rather than a list of atoms. The grid is the product of the two lists, so an arbitrary subset of it cannot be addressed at all.

Moving the atoms means sliding the tone frequencies. Change one x tone and the entire column it created sweeps across the lattice, carrying every atom sitting in it. A single instruction is therefore: this set of columns, crossed with this set of rows, moved to there.

So when a block of atoms shuffles itself around internally, that is not one instruction. It is several rectangles moving at the same time on separate crossed pairs — the workbook calls them AOM groups, and c300 needs four of them running at once. Working out how many there were, and which atoms were in each, is the entire difficulty of the job.

How it reads the movie

  1. It works frame by frame. c300 is 384 frames covering 7.22 ms — one frame every 0.019 ms. Every number in the workbook is measured on these frames, and every row says which frames it came from.
  2. It does not chase individual atoms around. Each frame is flattened into two one-dimensional colour profiles: add the picture up column by column, then row by row. Each species of atom has its own colour, so a peak in the column profile says atoms of this colour are at this column, right now. This is far steadier than tracking markers, and it locates a block to a small fraction of one lattice spacing.
  3. The still frames carry the answer. Between moves everything sits at rest on whole lattice sites, which gives a clean reading of exactly which columns and rows each species occupies. A movement is then the difference between one rest state and the next one.
  4. Gate pulses cut the film into pieces. When a gate fires, the movie flashes and nothing moves. Those flashes work as punctuation: they split the film into pulse-free intervals, and each interval is solved on its own instead of all at once.
  5. Then it solves for the rectangles. Knowing the occupancy before and after, it searches for the smallest set of column × row rectangles that carries one into the other. This is where the difficulty lives — the same before-and-after can be produced by several different sets of rectangles, and only the frames in between tell them apart.
  6. The timing comes from those in-between frames. Atoms accelerate and decelerate along a fixed easing curve rather than moving at constant speed. Fitting the measured positions across the intermediate frames to that curve pins down when each move started and finished, to a fraction of a frame.
  7. Finally it marks its own work. The finished workbook is rendered back into a movie and compared with the original, position by position. Every row in the schedule reports how far its rebuilt positions landed from the measured ones, in lattice sites — those are the 0.118 and 0.032 figures in the table below.

The movie runs at only 8 frames per second, so a quick move may be covered by just a handful of frames. When that happens the geometry can be genuinely ambiguous — more than one set of rectangles fits everything the frames show — and the Python cannot decide between them on its own. That is where Claude does the work: reading those frames directly, judging which reading is consistent with the rest of the cycle, and saying plainly when it still cannot tell.

B

How You Use It

You do not run commands. You talk to Claude Code, which reads the instructions in the package and runs them. Install it once by copying the two folders inside skills\ into .claude\skills\ in whatever directory you want to work in, then open Claude Code there. After that the entire interface is four sentences:

The whole interface
When you wantYou say
to start a movie“Transcribe <path to the .mp4> into a schedule workbook using the se-cycle-blocks skill.”
it to check its own work“Now apply the handmade correction method: find your own mistakes, fix them, and give me a new video.”
to report something you saw“Wrong movement between frames 176 and 184.”
to finish“That video is correct. Consider it closed.”

Why you have to watch the video yourself

There is a kind of error that no automatic check can catch, by construction. Before and after, everything sits in exactly the same places — so every automatic check sees a perfect match. Only the frames in between differ.

This was tested by taking a finished, correct workbook and deliberately breaking one movement in it, then running every automatic check on the broken file:

CheckWhat it said about the broken file
structural auditclean
atom countclean
render validation0 problems, “self-consistent”
accuracy score0.044 — a good score

All four passed. Anyone who trusted them would have shipped the wrong answer. So play the side-by-side video and look for a small group of markers that swaps places in the source movie but stays still in the rebuild, or the reverse. Note the frame number and say so. Do not check still frames instead — at rest the two always agree, and that is precisely the problem.

What “good” looks like: an accuracy score of 0.03–0.06 (a first draft is usually 0.3–0.8), zero movements left undetermined, all automatic checks clean — and, the row that actually counts, you have watched it and said so.

  • Claude will sometimes say “I don't know.” Some things genuinely cannot be seen in the video — where a block goes after it is carried off the edge of the screen, for instance. Saying so is correct behaviour; filling the gap with plausible numbers is worse.
  • Nothing transfers between movies. Colours, block sizes, grid size, timings, which edge the spare atoms wait on — all measured fresh every time. Never reuse a number from a previous movie.
  • Never edit the spreadsheet by hand. Several sheets are generated from the others. Change the underlying movement list and regenerate.
